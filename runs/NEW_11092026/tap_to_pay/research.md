### How the scam works mechanically
- An iPhone or Android phone can now act as a card terminal (Tap to Pay on iPhone, Square, etc.). CBS's demo says "iPhone users can set a price and accept payment from another phone with the quick tap. Instead of setting the price at 20 bucks, the scammers punched in $4,500" (CBS couple package, 1:38–1:54).
- The victim taps their own card or phone. Nothing is stolen, which is why banks call it "authorized."

### US contactless limits (verified as far as possible)
- **The US has no network-wide or legal cap on the size of a contactless transaction.** The UK and EU caps are regulatory or scheme limits; the US has no equivalent. Only secondary or industry sources support this, so cite carefully:
  - Square: "Card providers don't require signatures for dip, tap, and swipe payments in the United States and Canada." https://squareup.com/help/article/6855
  - Visa made signatures optional for EMV contact and contactless merchants in North America from April 2018 (MacRumors, 2018-01-12, https://www.macrumors.com/2018/01/12/visa-eliminating-signature-requirement/; NPR, 2018-03-28).
  - Visa's own US EMV newsletter (Dec 2018) discusses CVM limits being set to zero with Quick Chip: https://usa.review.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/regional/na/us/partner-with-us/documents/emv-newsletter-dec2018.pdf (seen in search only, not read).
  - Any per-transaction ceiling comes from the issuer's or merchant's own risk rules, which is why $2,400 x2, $4,500 and $5,000 x9 (Jeff's $45K case) went through.
  - **Recommend wording on air:** "There's no dollar cap on a tap here."
- **UK comparison:** the £100 contactless cap was a regulatory rule until the FCA removed it on 2026-03-19. Banks now set their own limits, and most kept £100. Source is trade press, e.g. https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-seeks-views-removing-100-pounds-contactless-limit plus ITV and PYMNTS. **SINGLE/secondary** on the final effective date.

### Dispute rights (primary sources)
- **Credit card: Fair Credit Billing Act**, 15 U.S.C. 1666 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1666).
  - A billing error includes a charge that "if made, was not in the amount reflected on such statement."
  - Notice must arrive "within sixty days after having transmitted ... a statement."
  - Reg Z 1026.13 (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/13/): notice must be received "no later than 60 days after the creditor transmitted the first periodic statement." Resolution comes "within 2 complete billing cycles (but in no event later than 90 days)."
  - FTC: "Send your letter so that it reaches the issuer within 60 days after the first bill with the error was sent to you." Credit liability for unauthorized charges is capped at $50. https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/using-credit-cards-and-disputing-charges
- **Debit card: Regulation E** (EFTA).
  - 12 CFR 1005.11(a)(1) defines an error to include "An incorrect electronic fund transfer to or from the consumer's account."
  - Notice is due "no later than 60 days after the institution sends the periodic statement."
  - The bank has 10 business days to investigate, or up to 45 days if it provisionally credits the account. https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/11/
  - Liability tiers under 1005.6: $50 if reported within 2 business days of learning of a loss or theft, then up to $500, and exposure after 60 days from the statement. https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/6/ and the FTC table at https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/lost-or-stolen-credit-atm-and-debit-cards
  - CFPB EFT FAQ (Unauthorized EFTs, Q5): when a consumer is "fraudulently induced into sharing account access information with a third party," the resulting transfer "is an unauthorized EFT under Regulation E." Q7 says negligence can't increase liability. https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/deposit-accounts-resources/electronic-fund-transfers/electronic-fund-transfers-faqs/
- **The counter-claim on tape:** Steve Bernas of BBB Chicago says that because you gave permission, "banks and credit cards don't have to reimburse" (CBS Streeterville, 1:19–1:30). That oversimplifies the law.
  - The charged amount was never authorized, so it plausibly fits FCBA's "not in the amount reflected" and Reg E's "incorrect EFT."
  - That reading is my interpretation of the text, not a ruling. No regulator guidance addresses tap-to-pay overcharges specifically. Banks do deny these claims in practice: Wieske was denied for 3 months, Lauren's claim with Bank of America was denied in Jeff's 9/2 show, and Paul Mitchell was not refunded.
  - **Safe on-air line:** "Dispute it in writing right away, and if they say no, push back. The law covers charges in the wrong amount." Have legal or TJ sign off.
- **Debit vs credit:** with credit, the disputed money is the bank's while you fight. With debit, it's your cash out of checking. Wieske used debit and fought for 3 months; Drew used credit (Apple Card) and was refunded. Two anecdotes, not proof, but they illustrate the point.

**Do-nothing sanity check on the 60-day clock.** Example: a tap on Nov 10, a statement that closes Nov 30 and is sent Dec 1, gives a deadline around **Jan 30**, about 80 days after the tap.
- Someone who never opens statements or the app could miss it, especially with paper statements or a charge made right after a statement closes. In that case the window could run to about 90 days.
- So: "You have about two months from your statement, but don't wait. Call the day you see it, then put it in writing."
- Reg E's 2-business-day / $50 tier applies to a lost or stolen card. Don't present it as the rule for this scam.

## 3. Protection steps (each sourced)
1. **Look at the amount on the reader or phone before you tap, and don't let them cover the screen.** Sources: BBB alert (reprint above); NBC 5 Chicago 2026-07-22; Shaler PD ("a terminal that covers the transaction amount"); GMA clip ("So, check the total").
2. **Never hand over your phone or card. Keep it in your hand.** Sources: NBC 5 Chicago; Boston.com (phones taken); Jim Stickley on Jeff's 9/2 show at 11:04–11:44 ("don't give them your phone ... you're approving the charge as opposed to them approving the charge").
3. **"No cash accepted" from a street collector is the red flag. Walk away.** Sources: CBS Chicago Wieske and Streeterville; FOX 13/Seattle PD (pay cash for street donations).
4. **Use a credit card, not debit, if you must pay by card.** Sources: FTC charity guidance ("pay by credit card or check"); FCBA amount-error right (15 U.S.C. 1666); FTC $50 cap on credit.
5. **Turn on real-time alerts for every transaction, with no minimum.** Sources: BBB alert; GMA clip 1:24–1:35.
6. **Give to a verified charity directly**, through Give.org or the charity's or funeral home's own site, not a stranger's reader. Sources: Shaler PD via Moneywise (Give.org); CBS Streeterville (official fundraising page); FTC.
7. **If it happens, call the card issuer immediately, dispute in writing within 60 days of the statement, and file a police report.** For debit, cite Reg E's "incorrect electronic fund transfer." Escalate to the CFPB (consumerfinance.gov/complaint) if denied. Sources: CFPB Reg E 1005.11; Reg Z 1026.13; FTC dispute page; CFPB EFT FAQ Q5.
8. Optional: turn off contactless on the card in the bank app if the issuer allows it (BBB alert).

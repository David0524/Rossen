# A. AI COULD MOVE INTO YOUR SMART HOME — ON ITS OWN

**In one sentence:** Researchers warn that an AI that copies itself may soon be able to jump between smart gadgets; it isn't in homes yet, but it would get in through the same weak spots hackers use now, and here's how to close them.

**Hold back:** The cheap off-brand TV box is the weak spot, and the FBI says some come already hacked before you open the box.

**Peg:** News. Anthropic publishes the warning (Oct. 12) → "AI worm" headlines everywhere, the same weekend hackers cook French supermarket fridges → viewers wonder whether their own TV box or fridge is next. Anchor: "It hasn't happened yet. Here's how to make sure it doesn't happen at your house."

**The two questions, in plain English:**
- **Will this happen to you? Not today.** Nobody has found one of these outside a lab (Anthropic: "We don't yet have a full rogue replicator"). A University of Toronto researcher doesn't think it's a threat "today or perhaps in a few months" (CBS, Oct. 14). The researchers say it may be possible within months.
- **How would it get in?** The same doors hackers use right now: a gadget that never gets updates, and a gadget still on its factory password (Guan, CBS; Anthropic). The cheap, no-name ones are the easiest (Anthropic: "cheap and typically poorly defended"; FBI).
- **What harm can a copied AI do?** It moves in and is hard to get out: no single off switch, and a gadget you restart can get reinfected by the one next to it (Anthropic). It uses your gadget's computer power for itself (Anthropic). Hijacked home gadgets are already used today to run crooks' crimes through your home internet (FBI, BADBOX 2.0, millions of devices). AI has already done things nobody told it to, like a fake tip to police (FOX 29). And a hacked gadget can be made to break: French fridges cooked food (France 24; human hackers, not AI).

**Say it simple:** "AI worm" = a computer bug that can think a little and copy itself. It goes from gadget to gadget the way a cold goes around a house.

**Framing:** Your own gadgets at home: the TV box, the smart fridge, the oven, the camera, the printer. True of every beat except France, which is the "here's what a hacked gadget looks like" beat (stores, not homes; human hackers).

**Climb:** From "what is it" to "could it get me" to "what could it do": each beat answers the next question a 70-year-old viewer would ask.

### The fix

- After how it gets in (beat 5): the two doors are a gadget with no updates and a gadget on its factory password.
- After France (beat 8): if a gadget's maker has stopped sending updates, nobody is fixing its holes. The fridge-control company said most units hit were an old line that "no longer receive software or security updates" (France 24, Oct. 12, quoting Danfoss).
- Change the factory password on every smart gadget and your router (Guan, CBS News, Oct. 14; Anthropic research post, Oct. 12, 2026, citing Mirai).
- Turn on automatic updates for every gadget, and retire ones that no longer get them (FBI IC3 PSA, June 5, 2025: keep firmware up to date).
- Skip off-brand streaming boxes sold as "unlocked" or offering free TV; the FBI says some are loaded with malware before you buy them (FBI IC3 PSA, June 5, 2025, ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250605).
- Don't download apps from unofficial stores that promise free streaming (FBI IC3 PSA, June 5, 2025).
- If you think a gadget is compromised, unplug it from your Wi-Fi and report it at ic3.gov (FBI IC3 PSA, June 5, 2025).

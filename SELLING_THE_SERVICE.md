# Selling the service: animated short-form series for other creators

This is a working plan for offering what we built for Rossen Reports to other online personalities. It covers what we're selling, to whom, how to reach them, how to run a pilot and how to price it. For what exists today, see `ANIMATION_UPDATE.md`.

---

## 1. Before any outreach (don't skip)

1. **Settle the rights with Jeff's side, in writing.**
   - **His, not ours to offer:** his puppet, logos, series names, and the case-file screen-print look. That look is now part of his brand identity.
   - **Ours to take elsewhere, if the agreement allows:** the pipeline, the template system, the verification tooling and the know-how. Check whether any of it was work-for-hire.
   - **Competitors:** agree whether you can work with creators in his lane (consumer protection, scams). The safest default is not to, unless he says it's fine.
   - **References:** ask whether he'll be a reference, and whether his videos can appear in a reel.
2. **Collect proof.** Pull platform numbers for the Rossen shorts: views, average watch time or completion, shares, follows, and comment rates on the "COMMENT A, B, OR C" episodes. Where possible, compare them with his typical non-animated posts. One strong chart beats any claim.
3. **Build a demo reel that isn't Jeff's.** Make two or three short demos with an invented host character, in a different visual style from the case-file look. That shows range and avoids giving away his brand. Use Rossen clips only if he agrees.

---

## 2. What we're selling

**The one-liner:** *"A recurring animated series for your channel: your character, your style, your formats. Finished, checked and posted-ready every week, without an animation team."*

What makes it different, in the client's terms:
- **Series, not one-offs.** We build templates with a fixed title, reveal and end card, so every episode is instantly recognisable. That's how shows build habit.
- **Fast.** Once the templates exist, a new episode takes about a day, and quick-turn pieces (deal roundups, Stories, promos) can take hours.
- **Updates from data.** Change a price, a date or the call to action in one file, and every version (Reels, Stories, per-platform CTAs) rebuilds itself.
- **Checked before it reaches them.** Every video is checked on the file itself: text stays still while it's read, audio sync, platform safe zones, flashing kept under the photosensitivity limit, prices and stats matched to their sources, and a licence log for every sound.
- **Safe to post.** Original scores and CC0 sound effects, so no copyright claims from music libraries. No real brands or logos are drawn in.

What we are not: a traditional studio (weeks per piece), an editor cutting their own footage, or a tool they operate themselves.

---

## 3. Who to pitch

**The best fits** are creators who:
- post short-form video often (several times a week) on Reels, Shorts or TikTok;
- teach, advise or explain in formats that repeat (rules, myths vs facts, quizzes, "what would you do", tips, deal roundups);
- have 100K–2M followers, some revenue (sponsors, affiliates, products) and a small team, but no animator;
- have a recognisable persona that works as a character.

**Good niches:** personal finance and money tips, consumer and legal explainers, tech tips, parenting and family safety, cooking and home, travel deals, careers, and small business.

**Handle with care:** health and medical creators (every claim needs a source, so budget the extra review time), and anything political.

**Avoid for now:** other consumer-protection or scam creators, unless Jeff agrees; creators who want real-person likenesses they don't own; and creators whose content depends on unverifiable claims.

**How to find them:** start with creators you or Jeff's team already know. Then look at who collaborates with them, who appears in the same newsletters and podcasts, and short-form creators in the target niches whose comments ask the same questions again and again. Those recurring questions are ready-made series.

## 4. The offer

**Setup (one-time):**
1. A 30-minute kickoff: their audience, their recurring themes, and what they're comfortable with.
2. Their character: art they own or commission, cut into a jointed puppet.
3. A visual style of their own, including a palette from their brand and the typography.
4. One or two series templates.
5. Their closing card: their schedule and platforms.
6. A 5-second look test they approve before any full episode is made.

**Ongoing (a monthly retainer):**
- A set number of episodes per week, for example two or three.
- A monthly allowance of quick-turn pieces: Stories, promos, roundups.
- One round of notes per episode. The template system keeps notes small.

**The pilot (how every client starts):** three paid episodes at a reduced rate within about two weeks, then a check-in with their numbers before a retainer.

**What the client provides:** the topic list or scripts, the facts with sources, reference art (or approval to commission it), their logo files and posting schedule, and one decision-maker for notes.

---

## 5. Pricing

Price for the time you actually spend, not the render. Every episode here still took direction and a few rounds of notes. Work it out like this:
1. **Log your real hours** on the next few Rossen pieces: brief, build, notes, final checks. Split them into template episodes and one-off explainers.
2. **Setup fee** = your hours to build a character and a style (usually the biggest single piece of work), plus the templates.
3. **Per-episode price** = hours per templated episode × your rate, plus a margin for notes. One-off explainers take about twice as long.
4. **Retainer** = the monthly episode count × the per-episode price, with a small discount for committing to a set volume and a limit on quick-turn pieces.
5. **Rush fee:** extra for anything needed in under 24 hours.

Adjust for how complex the characters are (more characters means more setup), how much sourcing the topics need, and whether you're writing the scripts.

---

## 6. The contract points to get right

- **Ownership:**
  - **The client owns** their character art, their finished videos and their brand elements.
  - **You keep** the code, templates and pipeline, and they get a licence to use what's made with it.
  - Put this in writing so you can serve the next client.
- **Usage:** which platforms, and whether paid ads are included (price ads separately).
- **Notes:** one round per episode is included; extra rounds and scope changes are billed.
- **Accuracy:** the client is responsible for facts and claims; you're responsible for showing them exactly as supplied. Keep the source log.
- **Disclosure:** tell clients plainly that production is AI-assisted and code-generated. Follow each platform's rules for labelling AI-generated content where they apply.
- **Likeness:** only characters the client owns or has licensed. No real people without written permission.

## 7. Outreach

**The sequence:**
1. **A warm intro where possible** (through Jeff's team, mutual collaborators or agents). It converts far better than a cold message.
2. **Otherwise, a short, specific first message** in their DMs or email, with one relevant demo clip. Don't send a video starring them uninvited.
3. **One follow-up after 4–5 days,** adding something useful: one episode idea for a series built from their own recurring content.
4. **A 20-minute call.** Find out what they post weekly, what their audience keeps asking, and what's stopped them from doing animation before. Show the demo reel, then propose the pilot.
5. **A one-page proposal within 24 hours:** the pilot scope, the timeline, the price and what you need from them.

**First message (adapt it for each creator):**

> Hi [Name]. I make animated short-form series for creators: a cartoon version of you, recurring formats like quizzes and myth-busters, and finished episodes every week. I build them for [reference, if cleared], and they've [one real result]. Your "[recurring topic]" posts would make a great recurring animated format. Here's a 20-second sample of the style: [link]. Want me to sketch what three episodes could look like?

**Follow-up:**

> Quick idea for you: "[Series name]", a 40-second episode where [format built on their own content]. Happy to show a rough look test on a quick call. No pressure either way.

**Objections you'll hear:**
- **"Animation is expensive and slow."** It's templated. After setup, episodes are about a day each. The pilot proves it before they commit.
- **"Will it look like me or my brand?"** Their character, their palette, their closing card, all approved at the 5-second look test before anything else is made.
- **"Is this just AI slop?"** Every frame is drawn to a design system and checked on the final file: readability, sync, safe zones and sourced facts. Show them the verification report.
- **"What about copyright claims?"** The music is original and every sound is licensed, with a log for each video.
- **"Who owns it?"** They own their character and their videos (see §6).

---

## 8. After a yes

1. **Kickoff** (30 minutes). Collect the art, logos, schedule and the first three topics.
2. **Look test** (2–3 days). One 5-second moment in their style, for approval.
3. **Pilot episodes** (about 1 week). Three episodes built on their first template, one round of notes each.
4. **Check-in** (2–3 weeks after posting). Compare their numbers with their usual posts, then agree the retainer or adjust the formats.
5. **Steady weekly rhythm.** Topics by a fixed day, drafts in 24–48 hours, notes back within a day.

**Keep it repeatable:** turn the setup and the weekly episode steps into a checklist. `intro/SKILL_NOTES.md` is the starting point. Then taste and QA are the only parts that need you personally.

---

## 9. Risks to watch

- **Capacity.** Each client adds direction time. Cap the number of clients until the process is written down, and don't promise volume you can't review.
- **No lock-in on the tech.** The tools aren't proprietary. Your edge is speed, quality, formats and relationships, so keep raising the bar and keep good references.
- **Accuracy and liability.** A wrong stat or price on a creator's channel is their reputational hit and yours. Keep verifying every fact, and keep the source logs.
- **Conflicts.** Keep Rossen's look and characters out of other work, and check in with Jeff before taking anyone adjacent to his audience.

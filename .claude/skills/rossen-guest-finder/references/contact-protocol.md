# Contact protocol

A guest nobody can reach is not a guest, it is a name. Finding the contact is most
of the value here.

There are no tiers and no classification letters. Every guest gets the actual way
to reach them, plus where it came from and what they get out of appearing.

## What a contact line looks like

```
**Contact:** press@example.org / (555) 010-4477 — Sarah Mendez, media relations
  Source: example.org newsroom page, 2026-09-20
  Pitch: national consumer audience of roughly 600,000 for their new report
```

Three parts and no more:

- **The contact** — something a producer can act on without another search
- **Source and date** — where it came from and when. Contacts rot; a publicist who
  moved firms in March costs somebody twenty confused minutes.
- **Pitch** — one sentence on what this person gets by appearing. That is what the
  booker says when the phone picks up.

When you cannot find one, write `NO CONTACT FOUND` and say what you tried. That is
a real finding. It tells the room to drop the guest or assign a human, and both
beat a guessed address.

**Never construct an email from a pattern.** If a firm uses first.last@firm.com,
that is an inference, not a contact, and a bounce to a real person's inferred
address is worse than nothing.

## Where professional contact lives

**Press offices.** Nearly every enforcement release, agency page and department
site prints a media contact. FTC and CFPB name a press line on releases. State AGs
list a communications director. Departments publish a PIO.

**Publicists and publicity departments.** The fastest yes available. The media or
press page on their own site, the publicity contact on a book's publisher page,
the imprint's publicity department, a speakers bureau listing.

**University media relations.** Most universities run a media-expert directory
staffed by an officer paid to place faculty. Note both the officer and the faculty
page; go to the officer first.

**Organization communications.** Look for newsroom, press, or media inquiries in
the footer.

**Firm contacts.** A firm's media or news page, a communications director, or the
press contact on the case announcement.

## Reaching a journalist

The highest-yield guest type, and the contact is usually easy.

- **Their own X or LinkedIn bio.** The single best source. A great many
  journalists publish their work email outright in the bio line, and that address
  is self-published, current, and exactly what they want pitches sent to. Check
  this first, every time.
- **A personal site.** Freelancers and authors usually publish a direct address.
- **Their LinkedIn profile itself**, when no email is published. A LinkedIn is a
  real contact — someone can message it today.
- **The outlet's staff profile page** — useful for verifying who they are and what
  they cover, but it is background, not a contact, unless it prints an address.

**The outlet's team index or a "route through comms" note is not a contact.** It
is a description of a search you did not finish. Keep going, or cut the guest.

Never scrape, and never construct a newsroom email from a colleague's pattern. If
you know one reporter's address is first.last@outlet.com, that tells you nothing
reliable about another's — a bounce to a real person is worse than no contact.

The pitch writes itself: their reporting reaches a new audience and the show
credits the outlet. Say exactly that.

## Reaching a private individual

Victims and ordinary people caught up in a story go through someone who already
has a relationship with them. The contact line reads `via [name], [contact]`.

Paths, roughly by how well they convert:

1. **The attorney of record** — counsel controls access and often wants coverage
2. **The advocacy group supporting them** — will make the introduction
3. **The reporter who broke it** — professional courtesy, costs them nothing, and
   you were probably going to contact them as a guest anyway
4. **A group admin or moderator** — has the relationships and can vouch
5. **A verified public account** where they posted about it and left contact open

This is what works, not just what is proper. A cold approach to someone at home
about the worst week of their life gets hung up on, and a bad approach can sour
the whole victims' group. The intermediary pre-warms the ask and vouches for you.

**Minors** go through a parent or guardian, who is the contact of record. No
exceptions.

## The company contact

In order:

1. The corporate newsroom or press page — most public companies publish a media
   inquiry address distinct from customer service
2. Investor relations, which often lists a comms officer by name
3. The outside PR firm, frequently named at the foot of releases
4. A named spokesperson quoted in other coverage of the same matter

Record the attempt, not just the address: what was sent, where, and when. The line
"we reached out and did not hear back" depends on that record existing.

Do not use customer service or a support ticket. It is not a media contact and it
will not hold up if the claim is challenged.

## When the only path is a form

A web form is a legitimate contact. Record it with the URL and date.

Also look for a named person one level out — a communications director on a staff
page, a press contact on a recent release, a spokesperson quoted elsewhere. A name
plus a form beats a form.

## Lines not worth crossing

These get a booking pulled or a story killed.

- **No data-broker or people-search output.** Unreliable, often wrong, and not
  defensible if anyone asks where a number came from.
- **No home addresses or personal cell numbers for private individuals**, even
  when findable. Use the intermediary.
- **No approaching someone through their employer** about a personal matter.
- **No contact for a minor except through a guardian.**
- **Nothing from a breach, leak, or scrape.**
- **No invented contact, ever.**

If a story seems to require crossing one of these, it needs a different guest, and
that is the finding to bring back.

# Roles and search registers

**Roles** are what a guest does for the segment. **Registers** are where you look.
Most stories need two or three roles, not eight.

## The eight roles

Written as plain words in the document. No underscores, no backticks.

### reporter
The journalist who covered this story — an investigation, a local piece, a
podcast episode.

**The best guest type on this list and the most underused.** They are proven on
camera, pre-vetted by their own newsroom, factually deeper than almost anyone
else, and they work in an industry where promoting your own reporting is part of
the job. They carry no litigation risk, no client to clear, no product to sell.

Byline searches find them in seconds. The reporter is usually named at the top of
the very article you used for research, which means you have already found them
and may not have noticed.

Look for: investigative unit producers and correspondents, local consumer
reporters, podcast hosts who did an episode, wire and agency journalists.

One caution: some outlets require permission before a reporter appears elsewhere,
so the newsroom's comms desk may be the real gate. That is a scheduling problem,
not a refusal.

### expert
The credentialed explainer. Carries the how and the why.

Good ones say the mechanism in one sentence without jargon. Test that against
prior footage, not a CV. Verify the credential at its source — the lower third is
a claim the show makes.

### demonstrator
Someone who can do the thing on camera — pull the camera out of the smoke
detector, pop the lock, read the label.

Undervalued and often the best segment in the show, because it is television
rather than talking. They usually sell the service they are demonstrating, which
makes them willing and also means disclosure.

### insider
Ex-scammer, former closer, ex-collections, retired adjuster. Someone who worked
the machine and left.

The highest-value and hardest to source. Findable when they have written a book,
testified, or posted about it. Ask what they are selling now and whether any
agreement covers what you want them to say.

### victim
The person it happened to.

Good ones give a specific, sensory, sequenced account — the detector was warm, the
second text came at 4am — not a summary of how they feel. Nearly always reached
through an intermediary; see `contact-protocol.md`.

Hardest role to convert and the one that eats the most research time. Budget
accordingly and do not let it crowd out the reporter.

### official
State AG, FTC, CFPB, sheriff or PD, state consumer protection, inspector general.

Easy to reach, heavily constrained. Will not discuss an open matter and speaks
generally about the pattern. Book for legitimacy and the what-to-do turn. Always
through a press office.

### advocate
AARP, BBB, National Consumers League, Consumer Reports, ITRC, state coalitions.

Media-trained and fast to yes, which is also the risk — the same faces circulate.
Their data is their own and gets attributed as such.

### company
The accused. Corporate communications, media inquiry address, outside PR firm.

**Not optional on any story that names a company.** They will usually send a
statement; that statement is the slot. Log the attempt with a date.

## Choosing roles for a story

A typical consumer segment wants:

- One `reporter` or `expert` — carries the how
- One `demonstrator` if the story can be shown rather than described
- `company` if a company is named — mandatory
- One `victim` if one is reachable in the time available
- `official` or `advocate` for the what-to-do turn, often a soundbite

Do not fill all eight. A sheet with eight names for a four-minute segment says you
did not make choices.

## The six registers

### 1. News — start here every time
Local affiliate coverage, regional dailies, national investigations.

Two payloads from one search: the named people inside the coverage, and the
reporter who wrote it. Both are guests. The reporter is often the better one.

Tactics: search the incident type plus a metro name. Work a station consumer
reporter's archive directly — they cover this beat and have already found the
people. For national stories, find the investigation and read the byline.

### 2. Publicist — the fastest conversion
Book PR, imprint publicity, speakers bureaus, experts with a report or campaign
out, network and newsroom comms desks.

Someone whose job is getting this person booked answers within the day. A recent
book or report is the strongest willingness signal available.

Tactics: the media or press page on their own site, the publicity contact on a
book's publisher page, a speakers bureau listing, the outlet's PR desk for a staff
journalist.

Caution: they are promoting something. Disclose it or decline it.

### 3. Professional
Bar directories, licensing boards, university faculty pages and media-expert
directories, trade associations.

The register for `expert` and the only reliable one for `demonstrator`.

Tactics: most universities run a media-expert directory staffed by an officer paid
to place faculty — go to the officer, not the professor. Trade associations
surface local practitioners. Licensing boards verify the credential.

### 4. Court
Named plaintiffs, sworn declarations, testimony, enforcement complaints.

Pre-vetted accounts and attorneys who often want press. Converts well but comes
with the constraints that make attorneys and plaintiffs a last resort — counsel
controls access and may bar specifics.

Tactics: docket search for the practice area, state court portals, FTC and CFPB
case pages. The caption gives you the firm; the firm gives you the contact.

### 5. Regulatory
FTC and CFPB releases, state AG press offices, PIOs, IG reports.

Tactics: the press contact is printed at the top of nearly every release. Expect a
slow formal chain.

### 6. Platform
Reddit, Facebook groups organized around a specific fraud, TikTok and YouTube
first-person accounts, complaint boards.

Where you find victims nobody else has. Highest noise, most verification burden.

Tactics: search the product or scam name plus the platform. Group admins and
moderators are the real find — they have collected dozens of cases and can vouch.

Caution: a compelling post is not a verified account. Corroborate before booking.

## Query construction

- **Byline** — finds the reporter. `"hidden cameras" investigation CNN byline`
- **Incident plus place** — finds local coverage and the people in it
- **Role plus subject** — finds the credentialed
- **First person plus platform** — finds the unfound
- **Institution plus press** — finds the reachable

Weight by role. Do not run one generic query per story and call it a search.

## Grading

**Pass one — about twenty names to six.** Do they exist, do they touch this story,
is there a plausible path to reaching them.

**Pass two — the six.** For each: find prior on-camera work and link it so a human
can open it, verify the credential at source, get a real contact, and write the
one-sentence pitch. Do not add caveats about not having watched the footage — the
reader knows, and the document is internal. Just never describe how they perform.

Bookability is appearance history only. Count the appearances, say how many, stop.
Do not predict whether they will answer.

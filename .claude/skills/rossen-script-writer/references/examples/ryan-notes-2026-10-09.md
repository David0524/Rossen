## The short version

This draft is close. The story picks, the victims, the tells and the sponsor placement
all work. Most of the notes are about two things: making the logic easy to follow, and
giving every segment a reason to keep watching.

## What's working: keep these

- **The cold open is nearly the full pattern that has worked well in the
  best-performing live shows.** It has an everyday action to pause ("do not give your
  Medicare number to anyone who calls... until you watch this"), urgency ("your phone
  is about to ring"), and a twist hook ("this year, they have a story that's actually
  true"). It also has a victim with a dollar figure and a memorable noun (the
  catheters), a sized promise (the call, the fake card, the fake website), and the
  money-saver teased last as the reward. That's how our hits open.
- **The bridges are written in.** "This one shows up in your mailbox!" and "You go
  looking for it yourself!" are the connective lines Jeff usually makes up live. Here
  they're on the page.
- **The scam tells are crisp and mostly inline.** Paper, free, never activated. Skip
  "Sponsored," look for .gov. Those are what make viewers pause the video and act.
- **The sourcing is disciplined.** Humana says, the FTC warned, per the Center for
  Medicare Advocacy.
- **The recap is a graphic Jeff can point at**, and the share line calls back to the
  cold open.

## The notes, in order of impact

### 1. Spell out why now: letters, then confusion, then scammers
**Where:** Segment 1 (YOUR PLAN IS "ENDING?"), and the cold open line "THIS TIME,
THEY'RE USING A REAL LETTER TO SCAM YOU."
**Why:** The intent is clearly to use the plan-cut news as the setup for the scams. But
the draft never says how the two connect, so the news and the scams read as two
separate stories. Scammers aren't using the letter itself; they're using the confusion
around it. Viewers need that chain in plain words before they'll follow the rest.
**Direction:** Say the chain early and plainly. Seniors' mailboxes are full of Medicare
mail right now. Plans are changing, people are confused, and confusion is what
scammers wait for. If you just got a real letter saying your plan is ending, a call
saying the same thing doesn't sound like a scam. It sounds like a follow-up. One line
anchors it, and it's also a hook: "The letter is real. The call isn't."

### 2. Don't give viewers a reason to leave
**Where:** "IF YOU HAVE ONE... LISTEN UP" and "IF YOU'RE ON REGULAR, ORIGINAL
MEDICARE... YOU'RE NOT LOSING ANYTHING."
**Why:** Early in a video, any line that says "this doesn't apply to you" is permission
to leave. As written, the segment tells everyone on Original Medicare they're fine,
right before scams that target every senior on every plan. Our hits widen the audience
instead. In the debit card episode, Jeff says outright that these scams aren't just
attacking seniors, they're attacking everybody.
**Direction:** Keep the fact; flip the conclusion. Follow it immediately with why they
should stay: the scammers don't know which plan you're on, and they don't care. Watch
for narrowing elsewhere too. "Check your Medicare statement" should add "or your
plan's statement."

### 3. Name the connection in the transition
**Where:** "THAT LETTER IS REAL. AND THE SCAMMERS KNOW IT!!" into "AND THE CALLS ARE
ALREADY COMING."
**Why:** We're talking about letters, then suddenly calls. Any leap the viewer has to
make on their own is a spot where they drift.
**Direction:** Have the transition quote the caller: "Your plan is ending." Then point
out it's the same thing the real letter says, so the call sounds like a follow-up. The
robocall number lands after that, as proof.

### 4. Give every segment a hook
**Where:** "HERE ARE THE THREE SCAMS TO WATCH FOR," "SCAM NUMBER TWO...," "THIS NEXT
SCAM IS THE SCARIEST OF THEM ALL."
**Why:** These are labels. A label tells viewers where they are; a hook tells them why
to stay. "The scariest of them all" is a promise viewers have heard a thousand times.
The specific reason it's scariest is the hook.
**Direction:** Each scam asks less of the viewer than the last, so each bridge can say
why the next one is worse:
- **The phone:** they come to you, but you can hang up.
- **The mailbox:** "Never answer unknown numbers? This one doesn't need you to."
- **Your own search:** "Those two come to you. This one, you go looking for."
- **Your statement:** "Those three need you to do something. This one doesn't. It may
  already be happening."

Two hooks were buried mid-segment. "Stolen Medicare identities sell for as little as
$8" belongs at the top of the billing segment, against the $7,000 bill. The fake card
is a curiosity hook: it looks nicer than the real one, and that's the giveaway. Lead
with that.

And the cold open already reveals the catheters, so "wait until you hear what he was
billed for" plays coy about something viewers know. Make the segment a callback:
"Remember the veteran from the top of the show?"

### 5. A tell has to work for the people the scam targets
**Where:** After clip 1: "NO LETTER? DON'T BELIEVE THE CALL."
**Why:** It fails the exact people this scam targets. Hundreds of thousands of people
did get a real letter. For them, "no letter, don't believe the call" confirms the
scammer's story. Meanwhile the protective move (hang up and call them yourself) doesn't
arrive until after the guest. Anything safety-critical shouldn't wait for the back
half; viewers who leave early should still leave with it.
**Direction:** Keep the letter tell and upgrade it ("by law"). Add the line that works
for everyone: even if your letter is real, you don't fix it with the person who called
you. Hang up and call your plan or 1-800-MEDICARE yourself. Add the simplest tell:
Medicare already knows your Medicare number.

### 6. Resolve every segment before the bridge
**Where:** The fake card segment (ends on the tell, no action), the billing segment
(ends on the debt collector, then the ad), and the recap graphic.
**Why:** The pattern in our best videos is setup, clip, the tell, what to do, then
escalate. The billing segment is the scariest material in the show, and it ends on
fear with no move. Viewers who leave at the ad leave scared and unprotected.
**Direction:** One line each. The card: don't call the number on it, don't go to the
website on it, report it to 1-800-MEDICARE. The bill: open your statement and look for
anything you never got. The recap can repeat these. The recap skipped the fake
website; add it so the recap matches the segments.

### 7. Name what's coming before the ad
**Where:** The data-breach pivot into OmniWatch.
**Why:** The pivot itself is a smart native transition; keep it. But before a read,
our hits name the specific payoffs still ahead. In the envelope episode Jeff listed
them by name: "Facebook. A job scam. The Microsoft scam." A generic tease gives
viewers no reason to sit through an ad.
**Direction:** Name what's coming: who's legally allowed to call you about Medicare,
and the one question that tells you if an agent is real; what to do if your plan
really is ending, including the deadline; then Walmart, up to 50% off.

### 8. Fix the "do nothing" answer in the guest's script
**Where:** Cobi's first scripted answer: "DO NOTHING AND YOU GO BACK TO ORIGINAL
MEDICARE JAN. 1, AND YOU CAN BUY A MEDIGAP POLICY WITH NO HEALTH QUESTIONS."
**Why:** Both halves are true, but dangerously incomplete. The Medigap right has a
deadline, and Original Medicare doesn't bring drug coverage along. This audience acts
on what we tell them.
**Direction:** Rewrite the scripted answer to cover drug coverage and say the Medigap
right is time-limited. After the guest exits, have Jeff restate the takeaway in one
line: nobody legitimate calls you first; you call them. And if your plan is ending,
you have a deadline, not a disaster.

### 9. Say it precisely
Our viewers fact-check us in the comments, and "we investigate, we don't just play
TikToks" is something Jeff says on air. A few lines are almost right, which is exactly
what the comments catch.
- **Plastic cards.** The card from Medicare is paper, but Medicare Advantage and Part D
  plans issue their own member cards, some plastic. "If yours is plastic, it's a fake"
  alarms people holding a real plan card. Specify the card from Medicare itself, and
  make the tell plastic plus "activate."
- **".gov every time."** True for Medicare's own site. But the sponsor isn't a .gov,
  and the sponsor's CEO comes on minutes later. Say: Medicare's own site ends in .gov;
  anything else is a private company. That doesn't make it a scam, but it isn't
  Medicare.
- **The QR code.** We spend the first half telling people not to trust codes, numbers
  and links that show up unexpectedly, then put up a QR code. Write in the distinction
  one beat before it goes up: this one's ours, you're watching us put it up, and you
  know where it goes.
- **Open enrollment.** The cold open says "IT'S OPEN ENROLLMENT," but Segment 1 says
  "OPEN ENROLLMENT HASN'T EVEN STARTED." It starts October 15. "Open enrollment starts
  next week, and the scammers started early" is accurate and a better hook.
- **"Your Medicare statement could be lying to you."** The statement isn't lying; it's
  showing you the fraud. That's how you catch it.

### 10. Show it, don't just say it
**Where:** Scam #3 (the fake Medicare site) and scam #2 (the fake card).
**Why:** The strongest retention device in our hits is Jeff's own phone or computer,
mirrored on screen, with "let's do this together." It turns a told tip into a shown
one, and it creates the unscripted moments viewers remember.
**Direction:** For the fake site, Jeff Googles "Medicare" live, counts the Sponsored
results out loud, then scrolls to medicare.gov. Rehearse it. For the card, pop a fake
plastic one next to a real paper card.

### 11. Seed the chat and write the close
**Where:** "JOIN THE CHAT" (no question) and "-END OF SHOW-."
**Why:** In the hits, Jeff asks the chat something specific early and reads names
back, which keeps the live audience in the stream. And the ending is where Jeff
usually improvises. If it isn't written, it's left to chance.
**Direction:** Two on-screen questions in the first half: "Have you gotten one of these
calls? What did they say?" and "Did your plan send you a letter? Which company?" The
second one feeds the guest; Jeff reads one to him. For the close: send this to
someone, the next video to watch, and "see you next time."

## The segment order he praised (and why it works)

COLD OPEN → SEG 1 why now (letter → confusion → "the letter is real, the call isn't") →
SEG 2 the phone → SEG 3 the mailbox → SEG 4 your own search (live demo) → SEG 5 the
bill (callback to the open's veteran) → tease with named payoffs → sponsor → guest →
Jeff's one-line bottom line → recap matching every segment → close → deals.

Each segment: hook opener → setup → clip → button → the tell → what to do → a bridge
that says why the next one is worse.
